# Story Director

Use this reference when turning a vague topic or script into a visual narrative.

## Normalize the brief

Infer a working brief with:

- viewer promise and audience;
- platform/aspect ratio and duration envelope;
- factuality and evidence burden;
- tone and emotional arc;
- material world: objects, spaces, tools, textures, and processes native to the subject;
- production, rights, brand, and accessibility constraints.

Ask the user only when an unknown changes meaning, rights, brand, cost, or scope. Do not ask them to choose metaphors, layouts, transitions, or easing.

## Build the story before scenes

A topic is not a story. Choose an engine such as:

- hidden mechanism;
- contradiction or reversal;
- process and failure point;
- constraint or bottleneck;
- evidence leading to a reveal;
- before/change/after;
- accumulating consequence.

Write a causal or logical spine. If the arrows are weak, the storyboard will become disconnected illustrations.
For film, chapter, and sequence progression, vocabulary, and the full neighboring-scene scan, use [Film and Sequence Direction](sequence-direction.md). Keep the hierarchy there; this reference owns brief interpretation and scene-local reasoning.


For each narrative beat, record:

- viewer question entering;
- answer or narrative function;
- viewer state leaving;
- relevant claim IDs;
- visual nouns, active verbs, quantities, and contrasts;
- the relationship that could become visible.

## Physicalize the idea

For every abstract claim:

1. State what the viewer should understand.
2. Name the real mechanism without metaphor.
3. Find objects and processes from the subject's material world.
4. Choose a visible operation: route, accumulate, drain, split, replace, reveal, block, assemble, decay, transfer, compare, or transform.
5. Write a mute thesis: “Without narration, the viewer can understand that …”

Prefer, in order:

`evidence/real mechanism -> functional process -> system diagram -> tactile metaphor -> generic symbol`

Do not let a metaphor imply a stronger causal or factual claim than the evidence.

## Scene boundaries

A narration sentence is not automatically a scene. Group words into scenes by visual thesis and continuous world. One sentence may need several states; several sentences may share one evolving scene.

Use this compact grammar:

`Anchor -> Action -> Consequence -> Launchpad`

- **Anchor:** minimum orientation.
- **Action:** the defining relationship changes.
- **Consequence:** the meaning lands.
- **Launchpad:** attention, object, direction, or scale is prepared for the next beat.

Compress or repeat states as needed. Never assign fixed time percentages to the grammar.

For long-form work, a conceptual scene may persist for tens of seconds when it remains one coherent world. Add semantic states or microbeats when they serve the evolving visual contribution; narration advancing does not by itself require a new image, motion, or state change. Reuse the world, assets, and camera logic rather than redesigning the whole frame every few seconds.

When several essential anchors are introduced in speech, their first visual read should usually follow the spoken order unless a deliberate reveal or suspense structure justifies otherwise.

## Concept search

For difficult or important explanatory beats, compare **2–3 low-cost, materially different mechanisms** before expensive sourcing or implementation. A different palette, camera angle, or layout for the same mechanism is not a distinct concept.

Select autonomously using clarity when muted, story-specificity, factual honesty, meaningful motion potential, asset feasibility, focal hierarchy and transition potential, difference from neighboring scenes, and the cost of changing the decision later. Stop once a concept clearly wins. Preserve rejected concepts only when they explain a useful failure. For the selection procedure and scene grammar, see [Scene Patterns](scene-patterns.md).
